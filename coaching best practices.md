# VEX IQ Coaching Guide for Teams 6502A & 6502B: Mix and Match Season

The 2025-2026 VEX IQ season challenges first-year coaches to master both competition rules and elementary pedagogy simultaneously. For teams 6502A and 6502B, success lies in structured lesson planning that keeps 4th and 5th graders engaged while developing robotics skills within official coaching boundaries. This guide provides research-backed strategies and a ready-to-implement 4-week lesson plan that balances methodology teaching with competition preparation, addresses mixed engagement levels, and maximizes limited robot access through strategic rotations.

## Official coaching boundaries and competition mechanics

The REC Foundation's Student-Centered Policy establishes clear lines: **students must design, build, program, and operate robots with minimal adult assistance**. During practices, you cannot provide robot designs, write code, build without student direction, or give step-by-step match strategies. At competitions, rule \<G2\> prohibits sideline coaching, robot work without students present, or interference in judging. Violations risk disqualification and award ineligibility.

What you can and should do: teach building techniques and programming concepts, ask guiding questions that develop problem-solving skills, share troubleshooting strategies, and provide resources for student research. The coaching philosophy centers on "teaching instead of telling." For novice teams like yours, some Yellow Zone behaviors are acceptable initially—demonstrating component assembly with student assistance, modeling organization strategies, explaining event procedures—but must phase toward Green Zone independence where students work entirely self-directed.

**Mix and Match game mechanics** center on building stacks of Pins and Beams to maximize points through color combinations. Each 60-second Teamwork Challenge match requires three drive team members: Driver 1 (operates 0:60-0:35), Driver 2 (operates 0:25-0:00), and Loader (introduces game pieces throughout). The mandatory driver switch between 0:35-0:25 remaining demands practice and coordination. Basic scoring awards 1 point per Pin and 10 points per Beam in stacks of two or more connected objects. Color bonuses add 5 points for two-color stacks and 15 points for three-color stacks. Beams act as wild cards, taking on any color not already present. The highest-value strategy involves creating three-color stacks with Beams in matching goals, yielding up to 48 points per stack when including the 10-point Beam bonus. Standoff Goal placement adds another 10-point bonus. Additional scoring comes from clearing Starting Pins (+2 each) and ending matches while touching multiple scoring objects (+2).

**Balance teaching methodology versus solutions** by distinguishing between concepts and implementations. Teach the engineering design process—Ask, Research, Plan, Create, Test, Improve—but let students decide which mechanism to build. Demonstrate how to use sensors, but let students determine when to deploy them. Share how gear ratios affect speed and torque, but let students calculate and select optimal ratios for their design. At competitions, students must explain robot functionality in detail during judging, so understanding stems from doing the work themselves.

## VEX VR capabilities for parallel practice

VEXcode VR solves the perennial challenge of multiple students with limited robots. This browser-based platform at vr.vex.com enables programming practice without physical hardware. Each registered VIQRC team receives a Virtual Skills Key providing access to the Mix and Match competition playground for both Virtual Driving Skills and Virtual Coding Skills practice. Students can work on any internet-connected device simultaneously—Chromebooks, tablets, laptops—with no installation required.

The platform supports Blocks and Python programming in the same VEXcode interface students use for physical robots, ensuring skill transfer. While virtual robot designs differ from custom team builds (so code won't directly transfer), the programming concepts, logic structures, and autonomous thinking absolutely do. For managing practice sessions, deploy VEXcode VR when physical robots are unavailable: while 2-3 students build mechanisms, others practice autonomous programming on individual devices. During robot rebuild phases, the entire team can develop autonomous strategies virtually. Students can practice at home between sessions, extending learning time beyond your 1.5-hour meetings.

The free version provides full functionality for basic use. Enhanced licenses ($199/educator/year covering all students) add class management, offline versions, and enhanced playgrounds but aren't essential for competition teams who already have Virtual Skills access included in registration.

## Structuring 90-minute practice sessions for maximum engagement

Elementary students' attention spans approximate their age plus one—roughly 10-12 minutes for your 4th and 5th graders. The solution: segment each 90-minute session into shorter blocks with frequent transitions and brain breaks.

**Recommended structure**: Begin with a 10-minute warm-up combining team check-in, goal review, and physical movement activity. Follow with 45-50 minutes of core work divided into three 15-minute segments separated by 2-minute brain breaks—jumping jacks, robot-themed movement games, or stretching. Next comes 15 minutes of team strategy or competition preparation, then 15 minutes of scrimmage or challenge time providing competitive practice. Close with 5 minutes for reflection, engineering notebook entries, and cleanup.

The segment approach prevents attention decay. After 15 minutes of focused work, elementary students naturally lose concentration. Brief physical breaks restore attention capacity. Vary activity types throughout: alternate between seated programming, standing/building at tables, and moving around practice fields. Use visible timers so students see endpoints approaching. The competitive elements—timed runs, head-to-head challenges, point tracking—create natural motivation and urgency that sustain engagement when abstract concepts might lose them.

**Station rotation model** maximizes limited robot access while keeping all students engaged simultaneously. With 4-8 students and 1-2 robots, create four stations operating in parallel with 20-minute rotations:

**Station 1 - Robot Building**: 1-2 students construct mechanisms or modify robots using the physical hardware, kit components, and tools

**Station 2 - Programming Lab**: 2-3 students code on computers or tablets, testing in VEXcode VR Virtual Skills and preparing programs for Station 3 testing

**Station 3 - Driving Practice**: 1-2 students operate robots on practice fields or marked floor areas, developing controller skills and testing programs from Station 2

**Station 4 - Strategy & Documentation**: 2-3 students work on engineering notebooks, analyze competition videos, research mechanisms, and develop game strategies

Rotate groups every 20 minutes with 2-minute transitions. Over two practice sessions, every student experiences all stations, developing building, programming, driving, and strategic thinking skills.

## Developing student leadership and engaging all team members

At ages 9-11, students enter Erikson's Industry vs. Inferiority stage, building competence through accomplishment while forming stronger peer relationships. This developmental window offers prime opportunity for leadership cultivation through gradual release of responsibility.

**Weeks 1-4** operate coach-led with heavy support: demonstrate processes while explaining reasoning, model troubleshooting aloud, guide students in using resources. **Weeks 5-8** transition to shared leadership with moderate support: facilitate joint decision-making, pose guiding questions rather than giving answers, introduce decision frameworks like pros/cons lists. **Week 9 onward** moves to student-led with light coaching: students run meetings, make design decisions, troubleshoot independently while you observe and ask reflective questions.

Establish rotating leadership roles that distribute authority while building diverse skills. The Team Captain runs meetings from agenda templates, facilitates group decisions, and represents the team at competitions. The Project Manager tracks timelines and task lists. The Communication Lead practices team interviews and manages alliance partner interactions. Rotate roles every competition cycle or monthly so all students develop leadership capacity.

**Engaging shy students** requires intentional strategies since robotics naturally supports quieter personalities through hands-on work and technology-mediated interaction. Assign roles playing to strengths: programming allows independent contribution at computers, design sketching emphasizes visual communication, building provides hands-on participation with less verbal pressure. Use structured participation protocols like think-pair-share (individual thinking time, partner discussion, then team sharing), round-robin idea generation, or silent brainstorming with sticky notes enabling written contributions before verbal sharing.

Create psychologically safe team culture through co-created norms: "Every idea is worth considering," "Questions are how we learn," "We celebrate trying, not just succeeding." Normalize failure by modeling your own mistakes and celebrating productive failures that generate learning. Recognize shy students privately first ("I noticed you figured out the wiring problem"), then gradually build toward public recognition, eventually supporting their participation in judge interviews with teammates.

Implement buddy systems pairing shy students with patient, supportive teammates for specific tasks. Use digital collaboration tools—shared documents, team communication platforms, engineering notebook contributions—allowing students to communicate through typing rather than speaking initially. Most importantly, establish multiple contribution paths: organizing parts, creating visual diagrams, systematically testing and recording data, photographing work for documentation. Students can contribute meaningfully without constant verbal participation.

## Teaching robotics methodology to elementary students

The engineering design process provides scaffolding for systematic problem-solving, but must be adapted for concrete thinkers ages 9-11. Use the simplified six-step cycle: **Ask** (define the challenge and constraints), **Research/Imagine** (investigate existing solutions and brainstorm alternatives with minimum three ideas), **Plan** (select best solution using decision matrices and create detailed designs), **Create** (build the prototype while documenting changes), **Test** (evaluate against success criteria and collect data), **Improve** (iterate based on test results).

Present concepts concretely before abstractly. Show real mechanisms before teaching principles. Use familiar analogies: "Gears work like bicycle gears changing speed and effort." Provide hands-on manipulation before complex explanations. Frame challenges as story problems connecting to relatable contexts and real-world applications.

**Chunk complex tasks** into milestone components: "Today we build the drive base" rather than "Build the entire robot." Celebrate completion of each component with visible progress trackers. Limit information overload to 2-3 new concepts per session. Use multimodal instruction combining visual diagrams and build instructions, auditory verbal explanations and peer discussions, and kinesthetic hands-on building and physical movement.

**Programming progression** follows developmental stages. Begin with unplugged activities—human robot games where students give literal step-by-step directions, understanding sequencing without computers. Introduce VEXcode Blocks with simple drive commands (forward, backward, turn), emphasizing sequence, timing, and distances. Add conditional logic incrementally with sensor integration: "If bumper pressed, then stop." Connect to real-world thinking: "If it's raining, then take umbrella." Progress to loops and efficiency challenges: "Can you do the same thing with fewer blocks?"

Reframe failures as iterations. Not "Our robot doesn't work" but "This is Version 1.2—we're improving it." Document iterations (V1, V2, V3) in engineering notebooks. Teach systematic troubleshooting: check power, connections, code, mechanical systems in order. Ask "What changed since it last worked?" Model your troubleshooting process aloud so students internalize the methodology.

## Managing mixed skill levels and personalities

Elementary robotics teams inevitably include novices alongside experienced students, dominant personalities alongside shy ones, highly engaged alongside distracted members. Differentiation addresses how students learn without changing what they learn—all work toward the same robotics challenge through different pathways.

**Assign tiered challenges** at multiple complexity levels for identical concepts. For autonomous movement: Level 1 programs the robot to drive forward 24 inches and stop; Level 2 drives forward until bumper presses; Level 3 navigates a square path using gyro sensor turns. Students work at appropriate challenge levels while learning the same core skill.

**Leverage peer learning** through strategic pairing. Near-peer mentoring (experienced students helping beginners) benefits both parties—students learn 30% more when teaching concepts to others. Create expert stations where advanced students provide troubleshooting help in their specialty areas. Implement pair programming with designated Driver (typing code or building) and Navigator (thinking ahead, spotting errors, suggesting improvements) roles that switch every 10 minutes.

**Role assignment** channels different personalities productively. For dominant students, assign specific leadership roles with defined boundaries, implement turn-taking protocols for hands-on work, and channel energy into mentoring or documentation. For distracted students, use shorter task cycles with visible checkpoints, provide role rotation maintaining novelty, and create active observer positions (timer, documenter) when not hands-on. For perfectionists, emphasize iterative design language ("Version 1, 2, 3" rather than "done"), set time constraints forcing testing of works-in-progress, and model your own mistakes and learning.

**Interest-based differentiation** aligns work with individual motivations. Students interested in art/design focus on robot aesthetics and engineering notebook design. Competition-oriented students emphasize driver skills, strategy, and scouting. Math enthusiasts calculate gear ratios and optimize scoring algorithms. Writers craft notebook narratives and interview scripts. Technology-focused students tackle advanced programming and sensor integration. While roles rotate ensuring skill development across domains, allowing specialization within rotation cycles maintains engagement.

The station rotation system naturally supports differentiation by providing multiple simultaneous activity types. Students self-select within stations—at Programming Station, some use Blocks while others try Python; at Building Station, some follow instructions while others modify designs; at Strategy Station, some complete basic notebook entries while others analyze complex competitive trends.

## Four-week lesson plan for teams 6502A and 6502B

This plan assumes Monday/Thursday sessions for one team and Tuesday/Friday for the other, with 90-minute sessions, 4-8 students per team, and one robot per team initially.

### Week 1: Foundation and exploration

**Session 1 - Team Launch** (Monday/Tuesday)
- *Warm-up (10 min)*: Team introductions with robotics-themed icebreaker (favorite robot from movies/TV), explain VEX IQ program and Mix and Match game through game reveal video, establish team identity (choose team name within 6502A/6502B designation)
- *Core work (45 min)*: Kit exploration scavenger hunt identifying key components (15 min), introduce team roles with role cards—Builder, Programmer, Driver, Documenter—and initial assignments (10 min, brain break), engineering notebook setup with team information, season goals, and first meeting notes (15 min, brain break), review game rules together identifying scoring opportunities (13 min)
- *Strategy (15 min)*: Watch high-scoring Mix and Match match together, identify three different scoring strategies, discuss which looks most achievable
- *Challenge (15 min)*: Unplugged programming activity—students give step-by-step directions for coach to complete simple task, experiencing literal interpretation and debugging
- *Closure (5 min)*: Reflection—what surprised you about the game? Engineering notebook entry documenting first impressions, preview Session 2, homework: watch game reveal video again and list three questions

**Session 2 - Building Basics** (Thursday/Friday)
- *Warm-up (10 min)*: Review homework questions as team, physical warm-up game (human robot following directional commands), introduce goal: build Hero Bot
- *Core work (45 min)*: Demonstrate proper building techniques—pin tool use, beam connections, structural stability concepts (10 min), station rotation beginning: Station 1 (Building) starts Hero Bot chassis following instructions, Station 2 (Programming) explores VEXcode IQ interface and creates account, Station 3 (Driving) practices controller familiarization with existing robot if available or cardboard template, Station 4 (Documentation) sketches robot design from instructions and takes notes on building techniques (20 min total with one brain break mid-point), team assembles to continue Hero Bot building together (15 min, brain break)
- *Strategy (15 min)*: Discuss gear ratios shown in Hero Bot, understand speed vs. power tradeoffs, identify where gears appear in design
- *Challenge (15 min)*: Test Hero Bot basic movements—drive forward, backward, turn in place, measure distances traveled
- *Closure (5 min)*: Team reflection on building challenges encountered, notebook entry with photo of progress, homework: research one Mix and Match rule in detail

### Week 2: Skills development

**Session 3 - Driving Fundamentals** (Monday/Tuesday)
- *Warm-up (10 min)*: Share research on rules, quick reaction game with controllers (press button when color called), introduce goal: develop driver skills
- *Core work (45 min)*: Demonstrate effective driving posture and controller grip (5 min), station rotation with all stations active: Station 1 (Building) completes Hero Bot if unfinished or begins intake mechanism exploration, Station 2 (Programming) starts VEXcode VR Virtual Skills practice on tablets/laptops, Station 3 (Driving) practices obstacle courses with tape lines, precision parking challenges, and timed forward/backward runs, Station 4 (Documentation) reviews driving test data, creates data tables, begins competition notes section (four 10-minute rotations with 1-minute transitions and one 2-minute break)
- *Strategy (15 min)*: Analyze driver requirements for Mix and Match—what maneuvers are essential? Plan driver practice goals for next sessions
- *Challenge (15 min)*: Timed trials—how many cones can you knock over in 30 seconds? Record scores for improvement tracking
- *Closure (5 min)*: Identify one driving skill to improve, notebook entry on Session 3 activities, homework: practice VEX Virtual Skills at home (provide login information)

**Session 4 - Programming Foundations** (Thursday/Friday)
- *Warm-up (10 min)*: Share Virtual Skills attempts and screenshots, demonstrate programming causing robot movement, introduce goal: create first autonomous programs
- *Core work (45 min)*: Programming basics mini-lesson on VEXcode Blocks—drivetrain commands, forward/backward with distance, turn with degrees, wait commands (10 min), pair programming activity in pairs of two: create program making robot drive forward 24 inches, turn 90 degrees right, drive forward 12 inches, stop (15 min, brain break), test programs on physical robot with modifications as needed (15 min, brain break), introduce sensor concept with bumper sensor demonstration and if-then block preview (5 min)
- *Strategy (15 min)*: Identify autonomous opportunities in Mix and Match—what could robot do without driver control? Brainstorm three autonomous routines worth developing
- *Challenge (15 min)*: Programming challenge—create shortest program (fewest blocks) that makes robot drive in a square pattern
- *Closure (5 min)*: Pair share—what was challenging about programming? What did you learn? Notebook entry with program screenshots, homework: design one autonomous mission on paper with pseudocode

### Week 3: Game strategy and custom design

**Session 5 - Strategy Development** (Monday/Tuesday)
- *Warm-up (10 min)*: Share autonomous mission designs from homework, brief movement break, introduce goal: develop team strategy for Mix and Match
- *Core work (45 min)*: Game analysis activity—calculate point values for different stacks (single color vs. two-color vs. three-color, with and without Beams, with and without bonuses) using math (15 min), watch three competition videos in small groups with specific observation tasks: Group 1 identifies high-value strategies, Group 2 notes common failures/challenges, Group 3 tracks alliance coordination techniques (15 min, brain break), whole team discussion synthesizing observations into strategic priorities (15 min, brain break)
- *Strategy (15 min)*: Define team's initial strategy—which scoring methods will we prioritize? What mechanisms do we need? Use decision matrix evaluating feasibility, point value, and difficulty
- *Challenge (15 min)*: Quick-build challenge—using only materials on table, create a mechanism that can pick up a Pin in 10 minutes (fosters creative problem-solving)
- *Closure (5 min)*: Reflection—what's our biggest challenge to execute strategy? Notebook entry documenting strategy with sketches, homework: sketch two mechanism ideas for priority task

**Session 6 - Design and Build Custom Mechanism** (Thursday/Friday)
- *Warm-up (10 min)*: Share mechanism sketches, vote on which to prototype first, review engineering design process poster
- *Core work (45 min)*: Detailed planning phase—team creates labeled design drawing of chosen mechanism with dimensions, identifies all parts needed, makes predictions about functionality (10 min), station rotation: Station 1 (Building) begins mechanism prototype construction, Station 2 (Programming) develops program to test mechanism once built, Station 3 (Driving) practices with current robot configuration working on Match strategy, Station 4 (Documentation) documents design process in detail with CAD sketches or labeled photos (four 8-minute rotations with 1-minute transitions)
- *Strategy (15 min)*: Test mechanism prototype—does it accomplish goal? Identify specific improvements needed, plan Version 2 modifications
- *Challenge (15 min)*: Mechanism competition—which design picks up Pin fastest? Time multiple attempts, record data, analyze what made successful attempts work
- *Closure (5 min)*: Update engineering notebook with test results and improvement plans, reflection—what did we learn about our design? Homework: research how other teams solved similar challenges (VEX Forum, YouTube)

### Week 4: Integration and competition preparation

**Session 7 - Competition Simulation** (Monday/Tuesday)
- *Warm-up (10 min)*: Share research findings from other teams, discuss what could apply to our robot, introduce goal: competition readiness
- *Core work (45 min)*: Integrate mechanism with robot and test full system (15 min), driver practice with timed 60-second runs including mandatory driver switches—practice transition between Driver 1 and Driver 2 at 35-25 seconds remaining (15 min, brain break), judge interview preparation—team practices explaining robot design, strategy, and design decisions to coach acting as judge (15 min, brain break)
- *Strategy (15 min)*: Develop match strategy card—specific step-by-step plan for 60-second match with role assignments for all three drive team members (two drivers, one loader)
- *Challenge (15 min)*: Full match simulation with scoring, identify what worked and what needs improvement, adjust strategy accordingly
- *Closure (5 min)*: Team identifies top three priorities before next competition, notebook review ensuring all work documented, homework: complete three specific notebook entries identified as gaps

**Session 8 - Mock Tournament** (Thursday/Friday)
- *Warm-up (10 min)*: Inspection checklist review—measure robot dimensions (18" starting size maximum), verify rules compliance, complete pre-competition checklist as team
- *Core work (45 min)*: Rotation through competition stations: Station 1 (Matches) runs three full 60-second matches with scoring and referee, Station 2 (Pit Maintenance) practices quick repairs, battery swapping, and troubleshooting, Station 3 (Judge Interview) presents to mock judges (invite parents or administrators) explaining robot, strategy, and teamwork, Station 4 (Alliance Selection) practices scouting other robots and discussing strategy (three 15-minute rotations)
- *Strategy (15 min)*: Post-competition team meeting—review match videos together, identify three specific improvements for real competition, adjust strategy based on mock performance
- *Challenge (15 min)*: Skills challenge attempt—how high can you score in Driver Skills? In Autonomous Skills? Record official attempts
- *Closure (5 min)*: Celebration of four-week progress with team recognition of individual contributions, final notebook updates, preview actual competition, motivational close emphasizing learning over winning, homework: prepare questions to ask other teams at tournament

### Between-week adjustments

**If teams progress faster than expected**: Advance to Version 2 mechanism designs in Week 3, introduce advanced programming concepts like loops and variables in Week 4, add additional autonomous routines, practice alliance coordination scenarios.

**If teams need more time**: Extend Week 1-2 activities into Week 3, keep simpler Hero Bot configuration longer, focus on driver skills over complex mechanisms, use more Virtual Skills practice for programming development.

**For mixed engagement students**: Assign specific roles with clear accountability, rotate leadership positions weekly, create mini-competitions within practice (fastest builder, most creative solution, best documentation), ensure all students present one component in Session 7 judge interview practice.

## Implementation strategies for two teams

Managing 6502A and 6502B on different schedules creates both challenges and opportunities. Leverage cross-team learning by having teams share engineering notebooks, watch each other's progress videos, and conduct inter-team scrimmages when possible. If practice space and your schedule allow, consider occasional combined sessions where teams observe each other's strategies, practice alliance coordination, and share solutions to common problems.

Document each team's progress separately but share coaching observations across teams. When 6502A discovers an effective mechanism design on Monday, introduce the concept (not the solution) to 6502B on Tuesday, allowing them to develop their own implementation. This parallel development provides you with natural A/B testing—different approaches to the same challenges reveal which strategies work best for elementary students.

Maintain consistent structure across both teams while adapting to each team's personality. If 6502A is highly competitive and strategy-focused, emphasize match simulations and performance tracking. If 6502B is more creative and building-oriented, dedicate additional time to mechanism experimentation and iterative design. Both teams follow the same four-week arc but with customized emphasis matching their engagement patterns.

## Essential coaching mindsets

**First-year coaching success stems from embracing uncertainty alongside students**. You don't need to be an engineering expert—model learning alongside your teams, use VEX resources together, and celebrate discovery. The REC Foundation provides extensive free support: STEM Labs with student-facing videos, facilitation guides, the Coach Training and Certification Course, VEX Professional Development Plus community, and active forums where experienced coaches share strategies.

**Focus relentlessly on student growth rather than competition results**. Teams attending their first competition learn exponentially more than teams who practice in isolation regardless of match outcomes. Even simple robots teach valuable lessons. Failed mechanisms generate more learning than successful first attempts. Your role centers on facilitating student-centered learning where 9-11 year-olds make genuine decisions, experience authentic consequences, iterate based on evidence, and build competence through accomplishment.

**Celebrate the learning journey with specific recognition**. Notice when students independently troubleshoot problems, make evidence-based decisions, collaborate effectively, or persist through failures. Recognize contributions across all roles—building, programming, driving, strategizing, documenting—not just competition results. Build team culture through identity elements (team name, colors, traditions), establish norms emphasizing growth mindset, and maintain psychological safety where questions and mistakes fuel improvement rather than embarrassment.

The 2025-2026 Mix and Match season offers teams 6502A and 6502B exceptional opportunities to develop STEM skills, collaborative capacity, and engineering thinking. By structuring practices around elementary developmental needs, balancing methodology teaching with hands-on application, engaging all personalities through diverse roles, and maintaining student-centered focus within official coaching boundaries, your first-year coaching experience will cultivate not just competitive robotics teams but confident young engineers prepared for future challenges far beyond the VEX IQ field.
